# The Life Behind the Work

*What matters most is a path of heart.*

An independent researcher and explorer, Ana brings a lifelong practice of making, tending and close observation to the body, the home and the places we live. Her writing, Circles and conversations grow from it.

It began in Unity, Saskatchewan, above a small-town pharmacy. Her father was the pharmacist, and a photographer. Lotions were made and measured by hand, and in the darkroom film slowly became prints. Her mother was a florist, and the early years were spent in the back room of the flower shop while she made her arrangements. That scent has never left. There was ballet, swimming and figure skating too, and the posture they taught has stayed for a lifetime.

In Yellowknife the basement became a goldsmith's workshop. Her father had gone back to school for the craft, and she learned it at his bench. Every piece began in wax. The wax forms were joined into a tree, sealed in plaster and fired until the wax ran out, leaving only its shape behind. Gold from the crucible was spun into that hollow. Something had to vanish before anything could be made.

Her parents designed and built their own house in the North, and renovated the one before it until it sold. The family lived inside big renovations. Walls came down and went up, and construction was part of everyday life.

The North was lake and sky. Family flights left from Great Slave Lake, just outside the house. From an early age she paddled alone and camped overnight, long before a phone could call anyone back. She worked as a lifeguard and taught swimming. Asked what she wanted to do for a living, she said, "I don't care as long as I'm outside."

On leaving home she went to the West Coast. She trained as a PADI Divemaster and ran a dive lodge, where the days were boats, tanks and compressors, and every dive was timed to the tide. She became a mother there. Crew boats and high-speed water taxis followed, through Seymour Narrows and Hole in the Wall, carrying people and freight to logging camps and remote inlets. The job required Marine Emergency Duties and a dangerous-goods ticket. Some loads were dynamite for the road crews. There were night runs, storms and fog on the radar, often alone and out of radio range. She was the one who had to bring the boat home.

The work was heavy, and her wrists carried it until they could not. She went back North to the family business, designing jewellery, and was certified as a diamond grader by the Gemological Institute of America. With a loupe to her eye, she learned to read how light enters a stone and how it finds its way out. Yoga came then, six days a week for two years. So did a year with Dominion Herbal College, some six hundred hours, that made her a Chartered Herbalist. The proof came later, in her own body. The first time a plant healed her, it was a shock. Feeling it from the inside confirmed what the books had only described, and the study has since grown into natural remedies and Ayurveda.

For a time she lived between the two places, returning to the coast each summer to work on the water.

In time she settled on the coast. The dive lodge became a guesthouse, and then came a house of her own, on a rock bluff on Quadra Island. She drew the plans by hand, draft after draft, and built it with John Seffzig. One intention guided it: "When you're inside, I want it to feel like you're outside." Light and air, wood and stone underfoot, the well, a crawlspace that could be reached to its narrowest corner. Christopher Alexander's pattern language later gave words to what she had done by feel.

In the end the house was sold, with nearly everything in it, and the travelling began. It continues today: shopping where local people shop, sharing kitchens, changing languages. Life on the road teaches what is worth carrying. Some would call it minimalism. It is closer to realism. There is no set list. What travels depends on what is moving through life at the time, and what is being played with. For the first ten years there was no social media, and then a computer crash took every photograph and much more besides. That was part of the journey too: letting go, again and again.

Early on, in a Buddhist medicine shop, a bottle she could not read turned out to hold fermented bergamot. Soon fruit peels were fermenting in jars that rode in her backpack, and Eco Enzyme was washing the dishes.

In George Town, on Penang, kefir became cheese, then a market table, then teaching. At the Vivarium, an apartment with windows on three sides, the air conditioning came out, and shade, screens and moving air took its place. A passion-fruit vine found the laundry wire.

That attention to rooms now carries into Place Readings, where a home and the life within it are read together.

Luang Prabang held her longest. In town, larvae appeared in the Bokashi bin. They were the Phoenix Fly, and alarm gave way to curiosity. For a year they turned kitchen scraps and humanure into dark soil, all in containers. The neighbours' hens came for the larvae and stayed to lay their eggs in that soil. An incubator followed, then hatched chicks and makeshift coops, and with so many chickens came a move to a farmhouse outside town. The chickens ate the larvae, and their eggs came to the table. The chickens themselves were never eaten. Soil, fly, hen and egg made one circle, and it changed her relationship with food.

Far from familiar routines, her wrists troubled her again. Shivambu arrived with a particular freedom: a practice available wherever she was. [That story is told in its own place.](/Ways-of-Living/the-body-as-home/beginning-with-the-bodys-own-waters.html)

She is a grandmother now, and family is far away. Missing people is part of this life too.

A window, a plant, a kitchen, a body, a conversation. The relationship with the living world is already there.
